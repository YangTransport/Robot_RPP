\maketitle\begin{abstract}

Humanoid robots are gradually being deployed in various service industries, including urban transportation. While vehicle automation removes the need for human drivers, many non-driving tasks traditionally performed by drivers still need to be addressed, creating a potential role for humanoid robots. This paper envisions a future scenario where humanoid robots are integrated into ride-parcel-pooling services, providing both passenger transportation and parcel delivery. We investigate the allocation of humanoid robots in a ride-parcel-pooling service, where the same robot fleet can perform passenger- and parcel-related tasks over time. We develop a mathematical model to optimize robot allocation while minimizing operational costs. The model considers factors such as demand patterns, robot availability, and task constraints. We also propose an algorithm to solve the allocation problem and demonstrate its effectiveness through numerical experiments. The results provide insights into the optimal deployment strategies for humanoid robots in shared mobility services, highlighting the potential benefits of integrating robotic assistance in urban transportation systems.
\\
\textit{Keywords}: Ridesharing, Humanoid Robots, Resource Allocation
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Autonomous driving is expected to remove the need for human drivers in ride-hailing and shared mobility systems. However, eliminating drivers does not eliminate the many non-driving tasks that drivers currently perform, such as boarding assistance for passengers with reduced mobility, parcel handover and verification, curbside coordination, and basic exception handling at pickup and drop-off points. As autonomous fleets scale up, this operational gap becomes increasingly urgent and can directly limit service quality and system throughput. In this context, humanoid robots become a practical substitute for driver-side labor, enabling autonomous mobility systems to support passenger- and parcel-related tasks without relying on onboard human staff.

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:robot_rpp}, the proposed task-allocation system integrates multiple mobility and logistics functions within a shared urban network. The diagram illustrates three representative task categories: passenger assistance, parcel delivery, and station operation. Vehicles, robots, public transport stations, and users are connected through coordinated routes, where solid arrows indicate vehicle or robot movements and dotted arrows represent station-related task flows. Passenger-assistance tasks support users with mobility needs at pick-up and drop-off nodes. Parcel-chain tasks enable robots and vehicles to cooperate in transferring packages across different locations. Station-operation tasks provide time-block robot support for public transport stations, such as passenger guidance, crowd management, and congestion mitigation. Together, these interconnected components demonstrate how the system can support both human-centered mobility and goods transportation in a unified operational framework.

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\label{subsec:rpp_preliminaries}

The ride-parcel-pooling (RPP) system studied by \citet{FehnEtAl2023}
integrates parcel delivery into an on-demand ride-pooling fleet, allowing the
same vehicles to transport both passengers and parcels. Passenger requests are
revealed dynamically and handled through an immediate
insertion heuristic that assigns each feasible request to a vehicle. Each passenger-request insertion updates a vehicle schedule and marks the
corresponding vehicle as having a changed route. Such changes will trigger the assignment of parcel requests to the updated vehicle schedule at the next RPP optimization time point, which is based on the pending parcel pool and these changed vehicle schedules. This
study uses the combined delivery and pick-up assignment (CDPA) strategy, under which the parcel
pick-up and drop-off stops are inserted jointly into the schedule of the same
vehicle. The resulting vehicle schedules remain subject to vehicle capacity,
passenger waiting-time and detour constraints, and route feasibility (see
\citet{FehnEtAl2023} for details).

\subsection{Robot Tasks}
\label{subsec:task_information}

Next, we introduce a unified representation of robot tasks that captures the operational requirements of passenger-assistance, parcel-chain, and station-operation services. Let \(N\)
denote the set of access nodes, where each node represents a passenger pick-up or drop-off location, a parcel handover point from vehicle to robot, or a public transport station. The urban area is partitioned into disjoint local service areas, each
associated with one node \(n\in N\). Humanoid robots can travel between nodes by robotaxi or public
transport, with \(\tau_{mn}\) denoting the travel time from node \(m\) to node
\(n\). All node-to-node travel times are fixed and known.


Exact door-level parcel origins and destinations are abstracted by their access
nodes. The RPP system hands each parcel over to a robot at an access node, and
the robot may walk between that node and the exact service location. The time
required for this local movement is included in the fixed task duration rather
than represented through an explicit pedestrian network.


All services offered by the third-party robot company are expressed through a
common six-attribute task representation. The company operates a set \(K\) of
humanoid robots and provides Robot-as-a-Service to ride-hailing and public
transport clients. At decision time \(t\), it observes the set \(I_t\) of tasks
currently available for assignment. Each task \(i\in I_t\) is represented by
\[
i=
\left(
q_i,n_i,t_i^*,s_i,\gamma_i,R_i
\right).
\]
Here, \(q_i\) is the task category, \(n_i\in N\) the service node,
\(t_i^*\) the desired meeting time, \(s_i\) the fixed and known task duration,
\(\gamma_i\) the penalty per unit of meeting-time delay, and \(R_i\) the task
revenue.

The task set comprises three categories distinguished by their operational
roles:
\[
q_i\in\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{C},\mathrm{S}\},
\]
where \(\mathrm{A}\), \(\mathrm{C}\), and \(\mathrm{S}\) denote
passenger-assistance, parcel-chain, and station-operation tasks, respectively.
Each category is described below.

\subsubsection{Passenger-assistance task \((q_i=\mathrm{A})\)}
Passenger-assistance tasks are real-time services synchronized with robotaxi
arrivals at pick-up or drop-off nodes. Assistance at the pick-up and drop-off
nodes is represented by separate tasks. Each task is released after the
ride-hailing request is confirmed and the corresponding node and scheduled
vehicle arrival time become observable. The desired meeting time \(t_i^*\) is
the robotaxi's scheduled arrival time indicated by the RPP system. If the
assigned robot is not ready at node \(n_i\) by \(t_i^*\), the robot company
incurs a delay penalty at rate \(\gamma_i\).

The fixed duration \(s_i\) of a passenger-assistance task covers both direct
boarding or alighting assistance and any required local escort.


\subsubsection{Parcel-chain task
\((q_i=\mathrm{C})\)}
Each parcel request generates two parcel-chain tasks that synchronize robot
service with vehicle handovers at the parcel origin and destination. Parcel
origins and destinations are known by the RPP system at the beginning of the
day and shared with the robot company. Once a parcel is assigned to a vehicle,
the RPP system communicates the scheduled handover times, activating one
pick-up task at the origin node and one drop-off task at the destination node.
For each task, \(t_i^*\) is the communicated handover time. In the present
setting, parcel tasks carry no lateness compensation, so \(\gamma_i=0\);
travel and handover times nevertheless affect whether the robot can complete
other revenue-generating tasks.

The fixed duration \(s_i\) of a parcel-chain task captures the local movement
between the RPP access node and the parcel door.


\subsubsection{Station-operation task
\((q_i=\mathrm{S})\)}
Station-operation tasks are preannounced service blocks that may be deferred
subject to a start-time delay penalty. They provide passenger guidance, flow
management, or congestion mitigation at fixed public transport stations. The
station node, desired start time, duration, and required number of robots are
known at the beginning of the day. Each required robot is represented by one
atomic task. The desired start time is \(t_i^*\), the fixed service duration is
\(s_i\), and a start at \(a_i>t_i^*\) incurs the penalty
\(\gamma_i[a_i-t_i^*]^+\), as for the other task categories. Once assigned,
the task remains in the selected robot schedule, but its planned start time
\(a_i\) may shift when later insertions update that schedule. The corresponding
lateness penalty is recomputed from the updated start time.

\section{Robot allocation problem}
\label{sec:mdp_formulation}
In this section, we first formulate the robot allocation problem as a Markov
decision process (MDP) in Section~\ref{subsec:mdp_formulation}. To solve the MDP, we introduce a profit-oriented rolling-horizon insertion heuristic to approximate the optimal policy in Section~\ref{subsec:heuristic}.


\subsection{Markov Decision Process Formulation}\label{subsec:mdp_formulation}
The decision model is defined from the information available to the
third-party robot company over a finite operating day. Decisions are made at
times \(t\in\mathcal T\). For robot \(k\in K\), let \(\pi_{kt}\) denote its
schedule of accepted tasks and their planned timing.
The fleet schedule is
\[
\boldsymbol{\pi}_t=(\pi_{kt})_{k\in K}.
\]

Let \(I_t\) denote the pool of unassigned tasks that have already been communicated to
the robot company and are currently available for assignment.  Let \(P_t\) denote the parcel requests whose OD information
is known but which have not yet been converted into time-specific robot tasks
by an RPP parcel--vehicle match. Because these known requests affect future
task arrivals, they are retained in the information state
\[
S_t=\left(t,\boldsymbol{\pi}_t,I_t,P_t\right).
\]

Each action selects a subset of the currently unassigned tasks, assigns them to
robots, and updates the corresponding schedules. Let
\(x_{ik}^t\in\{0,1\}\) equal one if task \(i\) is assigned to robot \(k\) at
time \(t\), and zero otherwise. Each task $i\in I_t$ can be assigned to at most one robot:
\(
\sum_{k\in K}x_{ik}^t\leq 1,
\).
A task that is not assigned in the current decision stays in the pool of unassigned tasks.



For each robot, \(\Pi_k(S_t)\) denotes the set of feasible schedules that respect
robot availability, fixed travel and service times, task precedence constraints
(e.g., passenger boarding assistance has to be before alighting assistance),
and tasks already in service, which cannot be moved. Let
\(\mathcal U(S_t)\) contain decisions $\nu_t=(x_t,\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t)$
that satisfy the unique-assignment and precedence conditions above,
\(\pi'_{kt}\in\Pi_k(S_t)\) for every \(k\).


The state evolves as new tasks and operational information become available
between consecutive decisions. New passenger tasks, parcel-matching outcomes,
and their communicated meeting times are collected in the exogenous
information \(\zeta_{t+1}\). Travel times and task durations remain fixed and
known; stochasticity arises only from future task arrivals and parcel-matching
outcomes. Let \(F\) denote the transition mapping that
updates the fleet schedules, the available task set, and the unmatched parcel
pool. The next state is
\[
S_{t+1}=F(S_t,\nu_t,\zeta_{t+1}).
\]


The robot company seeks to maximize its expected cumulative profit. Let
\(I_t^*\) denote the set of tasks assigned at time \(t\). The one-step profit
is
\[
G(S_t,\nu_t,\zeta_{t+1})
=
\sum_{i\in I_t^{*}}R_i
-
\sum_{i\in I_t^{*}}\gamma_iL_i,
\]
where \(a_i\) is the scheduled start time and
\(L_i=[a_i-t_i^*]^+\) is the deterministic lateness of task \(i\) under the
updated schedule. Revenue is collected once, when a task is completed, and a lateness payment is
incurred when a robot starts after the agreed meeting time.

Let \(V_t(S_t)\) denote the maximum expected profit from decision time \(t\) to
the end of the operating day. The Bellman equation is
\[
V_t(S_t)
=
\max_{\nu_t\in\mathcal U(S_t)}
\mathbb E
\left[
G(S_t,\nu_t,\zeta_{t+1})
+V_{t+1}\!\left(F(S_t,\nu_t,\zeta_{t+1})\right)
\mid S_t,\nu_t
\right],
\]
with zero terminal value at the end of the operating duration.


The MDP provides a normative representation of the sequential allocation
problem. Although an approximate dynamic programming (ADP) policy could be
learned from simulated or observed operational trajectories, a reliable
value-function approximation generally requires sufficiently representative
training data and careful validation. Such data may be limited during the
initial deployment of the robot service. We therefore use a profit-oriented
rolling-horizon insertion heuristic as a cold-start policy. It can be
implemented directly, preserves schedule feasibility by construction, and
requires calibration of only a small number of parameters. As operational
data accumulate, the resulting state--action trajectories can support the
subsequent development and comparison of ADP-based or hybrid policies \citep{bertsekas1997rollout,auad2026data}.

\subsection{Profit-Oriented Rolling-Horizon Insertion Heuristic}\label{subsec:heuristic}

We approximate the Bellman action by the deterministic rolling-horizon policy
\[
\nu_t=\mu_\theta(S_t)=(x_t,\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t).
\]
The policy uses only the observed state \(S_t\), maintains the feasible
schedule sets \(\Pi_k(S_t)\), and represents the unknown continuation value
by a forecast-based opportunity term. At each decision time, it repeatedly
performs two distinct steps. First, it identifies the best robot and insertion
position separately for every task under the current fleet schedules. Second,
it selects one task whose best insertion is committed. The affected schedule
and all remaining task candidates are then updated before the next insertion.
During this construction, \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t\) denotes the provisional
fleet schedule, initialized as \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t=\boldsymbol{\pi}_t\).

\subsubsection{Rolling horizon}

At \(t=0\), station-operation tasks are known and enter \(I_0\), but they need
not be assigned immediately. An unassigned task is carried forward together
with newly communicated tasks at subsequent decision times. At decision time
\(t\), the heuristic considers
\[
I_t^H
=
\{i\in I_t:t_i^*\leq t+H\}.
\]
Tasks therefore enter the active rolling horizon as their desired meeting
times approach. A task may be deferred only while doing so remains consistent
with \(\mathcal U(S_t)\). In particular, station-operation tasks are not
forced into robot schedules at the beginning of the day, but at least one
feasible future insertion must be preserved for each unassigned station
commitment.

The unmatched parcel set \(P_t\) is not assigned because its handover times
are not yet known. Instead, \(P_t\) and historical task arrivals are used to
estimate \(p_{nt}^H\), the expected share of new robot tasks at node \(n\)
during the next \(H\) units of time.

\subsubsection{Best insertion for each task}

For task \(i\in I_t^H\), robot \(k\), and schedule position \(p\), let
\(c=(i,k,p)\) denote a candidate insertion. The heuristic ranks each feasible
candidate minimizing the score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:insert_score}
g_c
=
\Delta D_c-R_i+\lambda\Delta O_c.
\end{equation}
The score consists of the incremental lateness payment \(\Delta D_c\), task
revenue \(R_i\), and future opportunity loss \(\Delta O_c\). Parameter
\(\lambda\geq0\) controls the importance of preserving future robot
availability.

Candidate \(c\) produces the provisional schedule
\[
\left(\pi'_{kt}\right)^{c}=\pi'_{kt}\oplus_p i.
\]
Position \(p\) may lie between any two not-yet-executed tasks or at the end of
the schedule. The relative order of previously accepted tasks is preserved.
Only candidates whose resulting schedules satisfy \(\mathcal U(S_t)\) are
retained in \(\mathcal C_i(S_t)\).

The first term in \eqref{eq:insert_score}, \(\Delta D_c\), measures the change in lateness payments
caused by the insertion. Let \(a_i^c\) denote the start time of the inserted
task, and let \(a_j\) and
\(a_j^c\) denote the start time of an existing task \(j\) before and after
insertion \(c\), respectively. Let \(D_c\) contain the downstream tasks whose
timing changes. Because travel and service times are fixed and known, these
times are obtained by propagating the schedule from position \(p\). The
incremental lateness payment is
\[
\Delta D_c
=
\gamma_i\left[a_i^c-t_i^*\right]^+
+
\sum_{j\in D_c}
\gamma_j
\left(
\left[a_j^c-t_j^*\right]^+
-
\left[a_j-t_j^*\right]^+
\right),
\]
where \([z]^+=\max\{0,z\}\).

The second term in \eqref{eq:insert_score}, \(R_i\), is the revenue earned by accepting task \(i\). Because
revenue does not depend on the selected robot or insertion position, it enters
the minimization score directly as \(-R_i\).

The final term, \(\Delta O_c\), approximates the effect of the insertion on
future service opportunities. Let
\((b_k^+,\ell_k^+)\) and \((b_c^+,\ell_c^+)\) denote the projected time and
node at which robot \(k\) next becomes uncommitted before and after insertion
\(c\), respectively. Define its forecast accessibility by
\[
\Psi_t(b,\ell)
=
\sum_{n\in N}p_{nt}^H
\left[
\frac{t+H-b-\tau_{\ell n}}{H}
\right]^+.
\]
The corresponding opportunity loss is
\[
\Delta O_c
=
\Psi_t(b_k^+,\ell_k^+)-\Psi_t(b_c^+,\ell_c^+).
\]
It increases when an insertion leaves the robot available later or farther
from likely future tasks. This is a continuation-value proxy, not a robot
travel charge.

For every task with \(\mathcal C_i(S_t)\neq\varnothing\), its best provisional
insertion is
\[
c_i^{(1)}
=
\left(i,k_i^{(1)},p_i^{(1)}\right)
\in
\arg\min_{c\in\mathcal C_i(S_t)}g_c,
\qquad
g_i^{(1)}=g_{c_i^{(1)}}.
\]
This calculation identifies the current best robot \(k_i^{(1)}\) and
insertion position \(p_i^{(1)}\) for task \(i\), but does not yet assign it.

\subsubsection{Task selection and schedule update}

After the best robot--position combination has been calculated separately for
each task, the heuristic decides which task is committed first. This
across-task comparison uses two measures: insertion regret and task urgency.
Regret measures how much is lost if the task cannot use its current best
insertion, whereas urgency measures how close the task is to its desired
meeting time.

To calculate regret, first define the score of deferring task \(i\):
\[
g_i^0
=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if task \(i\) may remain unassigned},\\
+\infty, & \text{if \(i\) is a critical station task}.
\end{cases}
\]
A station task is critical when postponing its assignment beyond the current
decision time would eliminate all feasible future insertions under the
currently known schedules. The second-best alternative is
\[
g_i^{(2)}
=
\min\!\left(
\{g_c:c\in\mathcal C_i(S_t)\setminus\{c_i^{(1)}\}\}
\cup\{g_i^0\}
\right).
\]
The insertion regret is
\[
\rho_i=g_i^{(2)}-g_i^{(1)}.
\]
A large regret indicates that losing the best insertion would substantially
reduce expected profit or schedule flexibility.

Task urgency is
\[
\eta_i
=
\left[
1-\frac{t_i^*-t}{H}
\right]_0^1,
\qquad
[z]_0^1=\min\{1,\max\{0,z\}\}.
\]
If only one task has an acceptable insertion, it is inserted directly. If
several tasks have acceptable insertions, critical station tasks are
considered first. If several station tasks are critical, the task with the
fewest feasible candidates is selected, with \(t_i^*\) used as a tie-breaker.
Otherwise, the heuristic considers the currently profitable tasks satisfying
\(g_i^{(1)}<0\) and selects
\[
i^*
=
\arg\max_i
\left\{
\alpha\widetilde\rho_i+(1-\alpha)\eta_i
\right\},
\]
where \(\widetilde\rho_i\in[0,1]\) is normalized across the current candidate
tasks. Parameter \(\alpha\) balances the loss of a scarce insertion
opportunity against proximity to the desired meeting time.

Only after \(i^*\) has been selected is its provisional best insertion
committed:
\[
x_{i^*k_{i^*}^{(1)}}^t=1,
\qquad
\pi'_{k_{i^*}^{(1)}t}
\leftarrow
\pi'_{k_{i^*}^{(1)}t}\oplus_{p_{i^*}^{(1)}}i^*.
\]
This insertion changes the selected robot's availability and may change the
best robot, insertion position, and score of every remaining task. All
affected candidates are consequently recomputed before another task is
selected. This sequential update allows one robot to receive multiple tasks
at the same decision time whenever the final schedule remains feasible.

The procedure stops when no critical station task and no profitable feasible
insertion remain. Unselected tasks remain in \(I_t\) for reconsideration at
the next decision time. Optional tasks may eventually expire, whereas station
commitments cannot be rejected. The assignments and updated schedules
produced by the repeated insertions constitute
\((x_t,\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t)\).

The policy has three tunable parameters:
\[
\theta=(H,\lambda,\alpha),
\qquad
H>0,\ \lambda\geq0,\ 0\leq\alpha\leq1.
\]
Here, \(H\) is the rolling-horizon length, \(\lambda\) weights the approximate
continuation value, and \(\alpha\) balances regret and urgency.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:profit_insertion} summarizes the complete procedure. The
per-task minimization in Lines~\ref{alg:best_candidate_start}--%
\ref{alg:best_candidate_end} produces provisional best candidates only. A
task is assigned only in Line~\ref{alg:commit_candidate}; the subsequent loop
then recalculates the candidates of all remaining tasks.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Profit-oriented rolling-horizon insertion at decision time \(t\)}
\label{alg:profit_insertion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Observed state \(S_t\) and parameters \(\theta=(H,\lambda,\alpha)\)
\Ensure Assignment \(x_t\) and updated schedules \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t\)
\State Set \(x_t\gets 0\), \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t\gets\boldsymbol{\pi}_t\),
and construct \(I_t^H=\{i\in I_t:t_i^*\leq t+H\}\)
\While{\(\mathrm{true}\)}
    \ForAll{\(i\in I_t^H\)}
        \State Generate \(\mathcal C_i(S_t)\) from all robots and all
        feasible positions in the current schedules
        \label{alg:best_candidate_start}
        \If{\(\mathcal C_i(S_t)\neq\varnothing\)}
            \State Compute \(g_c\) for every \(c\in\mathcal C_i(S_t)\)
            \State Determine \(c_i^{(1)}\), \(g_i^{(1)}\),
            \(g_i^{(2)}\), \(\rho_i\), and \(\eta_i\)
        \EndIf
        \label{alg:best_candidate_end}
    \EndFor
    \If{no task has a feasible candidate}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \ElsIf{one or more critical station tasks exist}
        \State Select \(i^*\) by the fewest feasible candidates and then
        by the earliest \(t_i^*\)
    \ElsIf{no task satisfies \(g_i^{(1)}<0\)}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \Else
        \State Select
        \(i^*\gets\arg\max_i
        \{\alpha\widetilde\rho_i+(1-\alpha)\eta_i\}\)
        over tasks satisfying \(g_i^{(1)}<0\)
    \EndIf
    \State Commit \(c_{i^*}^{(1)}
    =(i^*,k_{i^*}^{(1)},p_{i^*}^{(1)})\), set
    \(x_{i^*k_{i^*}^{(1)}}^t\gets1\), and update its robot schedule
    \label{alg:commit_candidate}
    \State Remove \(i^*\) from \(I_t^H\); recompute candidates in the next
    iteration
\EndWhile
\State \Return \(x_t,\boldsymbol{\pi}'_t\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Solution Approach}


\subsection{Bayesian Simulation-Based Calibration}

The overall methodological framework is summarized in
Fig.~\ref{fig:method_framework}. The three-dimensional parameter vector
\(\theta\) is calibrated offline using Bayesian simulation-based
optimization. For a given \(\theta\), stochastic simulation is used to
estimate the expected daily system performance:
\[
J(\theta)
=
\mathbb E
\left[
\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}
G\!\left(
S_t,\mu_\theta(S_t),\zeta_{t+1}
\right)
\right].
\]
Because \(J(\theta)\) is unknown, noisy, and expensive to evaluate, a Bayesian
surrogate model is updated sequentially from simulation observations. An
acquisition rule then selects the next parameter vector to evaluate. The
resulting \(\theta^\ast\) is fixed for real-time deployment; Bayesian
optimization is not executed when an individual task arrives.
